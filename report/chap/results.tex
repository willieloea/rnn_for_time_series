\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section compares the final forecasting results with persistence, discusses
the findings for each dataset, and considers the limitations of the comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:final-settings} records the model settings selected on
development data. Each selected model was fitted afresh using initialization
seeds 11, 22 and 33 and evaluated on the reserved chronological test period.
Table~\ref{tab:final-errors} reports mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean
squared error (RMSE) in original units, with the sample standard deviation
across those three fits. This variation reflects initialization on a fixed test
period; it is not a confidence interval for performance on other time periods.
Both smart-home temperature targets are reported separately, and the two imputed
test targets per room are excluded from scoring.

Figure~\ref{fig:final-rmse} divides each test RMSE by the corresponding
persistence RMSE, making improvements over that baseline visible without
combining incompatible physical units. All three RNNs improve on persistence in
mean RMSE for minimum temperatures, both smart-home targets, sunspots and AR(2).
For air passengers, persistence outperforms every RNN on both metrics. The
lowest mean RMSE among RNNs is achieved by Multi for air passengers and both
smart-home targets, Elman for minimum temperatures and sunspots, and Jordan for
AR(2). These are descriptive rankings; small differences, particularly for
AR(2), do not establish a statistically significant advantage. Rankings also
depend on the error measure: Jordan has the lowest RNN MAE for air passengers.

\subsection{Interpretation by Dataset}

\subsubsection{Air passengers}
Table~\ref{tab:final-errors} provides the clearest example of why a simple
baseline is necessary. For air passengers, Multi has the lowest RNN test RMSE,
76.290 thousand passengers, but its error is still 47.3\% higher than
persistence's 51.782, as also shown by the passenger panel of
Fig.~\ref{fig:final-rmse}. Elman, which ranked first during cross-validation,
does not retain that ranking on the final period. Thus, the architecture
selected as best on development folds need not remain best on later data.

The passenger panel in Fig.~\ref{fig:final-forecasts} shows that the seed-11 RNN
predictions underestimate the two summer peaks. The development data reach a
maximum of 505 thousand passengers, whereas the test data reach 622 thousand.
This requires extrapolation to levels beyond those seen during fitting, in a
series whose seasonal amplitude also grows. Persistence can track the changing
level using the most recent observation, although it reacts one month late to
changes. The RNNs' peak underestimation is consistent with difficulty
extrapolating this growth. It does not, by itself, establish whether limited
capacity, optimization, or the chosen representation is responsible.

Only 120 monthly rows are available before the holdout, and
Table~\ref{tab:final-settings} shows that the selected Elman and Jordan training
durations are 99 epochs, close to the 100-epoch search limit. These facts limit
any claim that training reached an ideal fit. Table~\ref{tab:final-errors} shows
that Jordan has lower MAE than the other RNNs but higher RMSE than Multi: its
average absolute error is smaller while its larger errors receive a greater
penalty under RMSE. A seasonal baseline, a trend-aware statistical model, or
transformations selected on development data would provide useful additional
comparisons. They were not tested here.

\subsubsection{Minimum temperatures}
For minimum temperatures, Table~\ref{tab:final-errors} shows that Elman reduces
test RMSE from 2.481 to 2.203~$^\circ$C, an 11.2\% improvement over persistence.
Multi is nearly identical at 2.208~$^\circ$C; their difference of approximately
0.004~$^\circ$C is small compared with the overall forecast error. Jordan also
improves on persistence, but less strongly. The minimum-temperature panel in
Fig.~\ref{fig:final-forecasts} shows that the RNNs follow much of the local
variation while smoothing some sharp changes. This is consistent with learning
predictable short-term dependence while leaving substantial daily variation
unexplained.

The selected Elman and Multi models both use eight hidden units
(Table~\ref{tab:final-settings}). This shows that the smallest tested size was
sufficient to give their best validation scores under this setup; it does not
prove that every larger network overfit. Training curves and additional
controlled comparisons would be needed to distinguish overfitting from
optimization variability. Furthermore, the 30-day input window is shorter than
an annual cycle. These results therefore do not demonstrate that the networks
learned annual seasonality explicitly. Their small seed-to-seed standard
deviations indicate repeatable fitting on this split, not guaranteed robustness
to other climates or future periods.

\subsubsection{Smart-home temperatures}
Table~\ref{tab:final-errors} shows that Multi obtains the lowest mean errors for
both rooms. Its RMSE falls from the persistence baseline's 0.1345 to
0.0332~$^\circ$C for the dining room and from 0.1347 to 0.0391~$^\circ$C for the
bedroom: reductions of 75.3\% and 70.9\%, respectively, visible in the two
smart-home panels of Fig.~\ref{fig:final-rmse}. Nevertheless, its bedroom RMSE
is only slightly below Elman's 0.0395~$^\circ$C. The dining-room result shows a
clearer numerical separation, while Elman's larger standard deviation there
indicates greater sensitivity to initialization in these three fits.

These large relative improvements are plausible for a smoothly changing
temperature signal forecast only 15 minutes ahead. The models receive a day's
history of both indoor temperatures, outdoor temperature and outdoor humidity,
whereas persistence uses only the latest target value. The two smart-home panels
in Fig.~\ref{fig:final-forecasts} show smooth rises and falls that a model can
potentially anticipate from recent changes. However, the experiment includes no
input-feature ablation or simpler trend-extrapolation baseline, so it cannot
determine whether the improvement comes from extra sensors, the longer history,
or the recurrent architecture itself. All three RNNs have the same inputs,
making their mutual comparison more controlled than their comparison with
persistence.

The holdout covers approximately one week in the same monitored house. Accuracy
on this short interval does not establish performance across seasons, other
buildings, or thermostat interventions. In particular, a 15-minute forecast
evaluated using newly observed inputs at every step is a substantially narrower
task than predicting indoor temperature several hours ahead without further
measurements.

\subsubsection{Sunspots}
For sunspots, Table~\ref{tab:final-errors} gives Elman the lowest mean RMSE,
16.793, compared with 17.834 for persistence, a 5.8\% reduction. Jordan is close
at 16.912, and Multi is less accurate at 17.515. The metric matters here too:
although Multi improves on persistence in RMSE, its MAE is worse, 12.778 versus
12.595. A reduction in squared-error sensitivity to large misses does not
necessarily improve the typical absolute error.

The 132-month input window (Table~\ref{tab:split-settings}) makes roughly one
solar cycle available to each prediction. The sunspot panel of
Fig.~\ref{fig:final-forecasts} illustrates the one-step predictions tracking
much of the changing level while missing some abrupt month-to-month changes.
However, the target remains the next month's value, and persistence is already a
strong comparator. The modest improvement does not establish that the networks
can predict the timing or amplitude of an entire future solar cycle. The test
period spans about two nominal cycles, which also limits evidence about
performance across different long-term solar regimes.

\subsubsection{Synthetic AR(2)}
For AR(2), the three mean test RMSEs in Table~\ref{tab:final-errors} lie between
1.0185 and 1.0195, compared with 1.0868 for persistence. Jordan's numerical lead
corresponds to a 6.3\% improvement over persistence, but the separation between
architectures is smaller than their individual seed-to-seed standard deviations.
Cross-validation slightly favoured Multi instead. The nearly coincident markers
in the AR(2) panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:final-rmse} illustrate this small
separation, while its forecast panel in Fig.~\ref{fig:final-forecasts} shows
similar prediction trajectories for the displayed seed. These near-ties provide
little evidence for a practically meaningful architecture advantage on this
task.

This is consistent with the deliberately simple generating process: the
conditional mean depends linearly on only the two supplied observations. With
independent innovations of variance one, the known-coefficient conditional-mean
predictor has population MSE one and hence population RMSE one. The RNN errors
are close to this theoretical reference. However, it is not an exact lower bound
for an empirical RMSE on a finite test sample, and the oracle's realized test
error has not been computed. Only one generated realization and one coefficient
setting are used, so the result should not be generalized to arbitrary
stationary processes or long-memory problems.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations of the Comparison}
The experiment compares complete training configurations rather than isolating
feedback structure alone. Hidden sizes are selected separately for each
architecture, and equal hidden sizes do not imply equal numbers of parameters.
For example, Table~\ref{tab:final-settings} shows that the smart-home Jordan
model has 290 trainable parameters, compared with 1,250 for Elman and 1,314 for
Multi. Its competitive errors therefore represent a potentially useful
accuracy-size trade-off for embedded applications. Parameter counts alone do not
establish inference latency, energy consumption or memory requirements on a
target device; no embedded deployment benchmark was performed.

The search is limited to hidden sizes 8, 16 and 32, one hidden layer, fixed
input windows and a shared optimizer configuration. Cross-validation uses one
initialization seed, while the final fits use three. Consequently,
hyperparameter selection may itself depend on initialization, and the final
standard deviations do not quantify that selection uncertainty. Sliding-window
validation is supported but was not evaluated. A different training window,
forecast horizon or training budget could alter the rankings. For each model,
the final epoch count is derived from inner early-stopping results; it is not
selected using test loss and is not guaranteed to be optimal for the full
development period.

The models reset recurrent context for every input window. The reported task is
therefore prediction from a finite recent history, with actual observations
becoming available before each subsequent forecast. It does not test persistent
state across the entire series or recursive forecasting without new
observations. Data quality also constrains interpretation: masking avoids
scoring explicitly imputed targets, but forward-filled values can still occur in
input histories, and retained source values may themselves be uncertain or
provisional.

Finally, the test errors share timestamps and are temporally dependent, and the
three seeds all use the same data split. No formal significance test was
performed; neither a small difference in means nor overlap of seed standard
deviations establishes significance or equivalence. The fixed-seed excerpts in
Fig.~\ref{fig:final-forecasts} illustrate behaviour but cannot replace the
full-period scores in Table~\ref{tab:final-errors}. Additional chronological
holdouts, more initialization seeds, stronger non-neural baselines and
matched-parameter comparisons would strengthen future conclusions. Within the
present design, no architecture is uniformly best: the relative benefit depends
on the dataset, target and error measure, and persistence remains the strongest
tested method for air passengers.
